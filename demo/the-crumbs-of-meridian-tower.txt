THE CRUMBS OF MERIDIAN TOWER
by Marta Lindqvist


Prologue

At half past six the canteen on the ground floor of Meridian Tower closed its shutters, and the building began to breathe differently.

Upstairs, the last of the consultants switched off their screens and left their half-drunk coffees to go cold beside keyboards. The lifts sank one by one to the lobby and stayed there. The cleaners came at eight with their humming trolleys and went at ten. After that there was only the night guard, Dariusz, who watched football on his phone at the front desk and did his rounds at midnight and at four, never in between.

Twenty-two floors of glass held the evening light for a while and then gave it up.

Below all of this, below the lobby and the car park and the second car park, below even the room where the building kept its boilers, there was a narrow duct where the power cables ran. It was dry there, and warm, because the cables hummed with the work of keeping a tower alive. In the duct, behind a loose panel nobody had opened in eleven years, there was a nest made of shredded payslips, a blue lanyard, and the stuffing from a chair that had been thrown away in 2019.

In the nest, two mice woke up.

At half past six a crumb of focaccia fell from a tray in the canteen kitchen, rolled under the dishwasher, and lay there, waiting. It was always like this. The tower fed them without knowing it, the way a river feeds the fish.


Chapter 1: The Warm Dark

Tuck woke first, as he always did, because he slept lightly and worried in his sleep. He lay still and listened to the cables. Their hum had a pitch he knew the way you know a voice, and tonight it was the ordinary voice, the evening voice. No drills. No footsteps on the service stair. No dogs.

Beside him Bramble was already stretching, her whole body a long brown question mark.

"Lasagne," she said, before her eyes were open. "It's Thursday. Thursday is lasagne."

"Wednesday," said Tuck.

"Thursday. I counted the bins."

She was right, of course. Bramble counted everything that interested her and nothing that didn't, which was why she knew the canteen menu by heart and had never once remembered where the dangerous gap behind the boiler was. Tuck remembered for both of them. That was how it had always been, since they were pinkies in a nest behind the photocopier on the fourth floor, before the fourth floor was renovated and they had been carried down, one at a time, in their mother's mouth, into the warm dark.

Their mother was gone now. She had gone up to the fourth floor one winter to see what the renovation had made of it, and had not come back. Tuck did not think about it often. He thought about it every time Bramble said the word "up."

"Come on," said Bramble. "Before the Duster comes."

The Duster was what they called the night cleaner's machine, a round, blind, patient thing that went over the canteen floor every night at nine and ate everything that had fallen. Tuck thought it was the most frightening creature in the tower. Bramble thought it was rude.

***

The way up was a route they had made their own over three years. Out of the duct through the gap where the cable bundle bent. Along the top of the pipe that carried hot water to the radiators, which was warm enough to make your paws tingle. Up the inside of the wall where the plasterboard did not quite meet the concrete, a climb of four metres that Tuck still did with his teeth clenched. Through the cracked skirting board behind the canteen's walk-in fridge.

And then: the kitchen.

The kitchen at night was a cathedral of steel. The worktops shone with the green light of the emergency exit sign, and the extractor fans ticked as they cooled. Everything smelled of onions, of bleach, of bread, of the thousand meals the tower had eaten that day.

Mrs. Okafor ran the kitchen. Tuck had never seen her, because she left at seven and they never came up before eight, but he knew her the way you know weather. She was clean. She was fierce. She covered everything, wiped everything, and kept the lids of her bins pressed down with a brick. She was, Bramble said, their greatest enemy and their greatest benefactor, because Mrs. Okafor cooked too much every single day and could not bear to see food wasted, so she wrapped the leftovers for the cleaners, and the cleaners were not as careful as she was.

Tonight the cleaners had left a foil tray of lasagne on the low shelf beside the staff lockers, with the foil peeled back at one corner.

Bramble went straight at it. Tuck went round the edge of the room first, once, the way he always did, checking the dark under the dishwasher and the gap beneath the door to the dining hall. Only when he was sure did he join her.

The lasagne was cold and the pasta had gone leathery at the edges, but the sauce underneath was soft and rich with meat. They ate without speaking. Then Bramble found the focaccia crumb under the dishwasher, the one that had fallen at half past six, and she brought it to Tuck like a gift, and they shared it.

"This," said Bramble, with her mouth full, "is the best place in the whole world."

"Yes," said Tuck.

"Do you think it really is, though? The best place? How would we know?"

Tuck didn't answer. He had heard the beginning of this conversation before, and he knew where it went.


Chapter 2: The Light Under the Door

The loading dock was at the back of the tower, at the bottom of a ramp where lorries came in the morning with crates of vegetables, boxes of paper, bottled water, and once, memorably, a whole pallet of chocolate biscuits for a conference. A great grey roller door closed it off from the outside. The door did not quite reach the ground. There was a gap beneath it, the height of two mice standing on each other's shoulders, and through that gap, at certain hours, came the light.

Bramble had found it in the spring. Now she went there most nights after they had eaten, and lay with her chin on the cold concrete, and looked.

Tuck went with her because he did not like her going alone. He lay beside her and looked too, and what he saw frightened him.

There was a road out there, wet and black, and things moved on it that were bigger than the lorries and much faster, with eyes that blazed white in front and burned red behind. There was a sky, which was not a ceiling but a kind of endless orange smear, and it was never dark the way the duct was dark. There were noises that did not repeat, that had no pattern he could learn. And there was a smell that came in under the door on the wind: wet leaves, petrol, something green and sharp, and beneath all of it something enormous and cold that Bramble said was the river.

"The river," she said, as if it were the name of a lover. "Old Ludo says it goes all the way to the sea."

"Old Ludo says a lot of things."

"He says on the other side of the road there's a park. With grass. Real grass, Tuck. Not the plastic stuff in the lobby. And trees you can climb that go up higher than the tower."

"Nothing goes up higher than the tower."

"How do you know?"

***

Kev drove the vegetable lorry. He arrived every morning except Sunday at a quarter to six, rolled the door up with a long hooked pole, and whistled while he unloaded. He was the only human the mice knew by name, because he talked to himself all the time and said his own name a great deal: "Come on, Kev. Nearly there, Kev. Kevin, you absolute legend."

Once, a carrot had fallen from his trolley and rolled to the edge of the ramp, and Kev had looked at it, looked around, and kicked it gently towards the corner where the mice were hiding. Bramble had been sure, ever since, that he had seen them and done it on purpose. Tuck thought he had just been tidying.

That morning they watched from behind a stack of flattened cardboard as the door went up and the light came in all at once, white and enormous, and the cold rolled across the floor like water. Tuck pressed himself flat. Bramble lifted her nose and breathed it in.

"Tomorrow," she whispered. "Or the day after. When the door goes up, we just walk out."

"And how do we walk back in?"

"The same way. Every morning at a quarter to six. It's like a clock, Tuck. You love clocks."

***

Old Ludo lived in the drain beneath the car park, in a chamber where the floodwater came in when it rained hard and went out again when it stopped. He was a rat, and very old, and missing most of one ear and the end of his tail. He had come from outside, years ago, and he was the only creature either of them knew who had.

He did not like visitors, but he liked cheese, and Bramble had brought him a corner of Mrs. Okafor's Friday Cheddar.

"Out," said Old Ludo, when she had explained. He chewed slowly, his yellow teeth working. "You want to go out."

"Just to see," said Bramble. "Just the park. And the river."

"Little mouse," said Old Ludo, "listen to me, because I'll only say it once and then I'll eat your cheese and go to sleep. Out there, the ground moves. The cars don't stop and they don't see you and they don't care. Out there, the cold comes at night and gets inside you, and you can't get it out again, not with all the shivering in the world. Out there, there's no Mrs. Okafor. Nobody makes too much lasagne. You eat what you find, and mostly you don't find anything."

"But—"

"And there are cats," said Old Ludo. "Do you know what a cat is?"

Neither of them did.

Old Ludo turned his head so that they could see the place where his ear had been.

"A cat," he said, "is what happens to you while you're looking at something beautiful."

He ate the rest of the cheese, curled into his damp corner, and would say nothing more.


Chapter 3: Droppings

It was Mrs. Okafor who found them, on a Monday, which was the worst possible day, because on Mondays the kitchen was inspected.

Tuck learned about it the way he learned about most things in the tower: through the plasterboard, from behind the fridge, with his heart going like a moth against glass.

"Look at this," said Mrs. Okafor's voice. It was deeper than he had expected, and very angry. "Look. Here. And here. And here, on my shelf, by the lockers."

"Ah," said another voice, a man's. "Yes. I see."

"Do you see, Mr. Brandt? Do you really? Because I have been telling you since August. I said there is something in the walls. I said I hear it. And you said old buildings make noises, Mrs. Okafor."

"Old buildings do make noises."

"Old buildings do not make these." There was the sound of something being tapped with a fingernail. "This is not a noise. This is mouse. This is mouse droppings, on the shelf where I put the food for the cleaners, and in two days the inspectors come back, and if they see this, I am closed. My kitchen is closed. And who feeds your consultants then?"

There was a long silence.

"I'll call someone," said Mr. Brandt.

"Today."

"Today, yes. I'll call today."

Tuck crept back down the wall in the dark. When he reached the nest Bramble was awake, waiting for him, and he told her what he had heard.

She did not seem worried.

"Droppings," she said. "Is that all? She's angry because we're messy. We'll be tidier. We'll go somewhere else to do it. Near the boiler."

"He said he would call someone."

"Who?"

Tuck didn't know. That was what frightened him.

***

That night the lasagne tray was gone from the low shelf. The bins had two bricks on them instead of one. Someone had sprayed something around the base of the lockers that smelled sharp and wrong and made both of them sneeze, and under the dishwasher, where the crumbs had always rolled, there was nothing at all. Someone had pulled the dishwasher out and cleaned behind it, and pushed it back.

They went home hungry.

Bramble lay in the nest with her back to him, and he could tell by her breathing that she was not asleep.

"It's a sign," she said finally.

"It's the inspection."

"It's a sign, Tuck. We've been here our whole lives. We've never seen the sky except through a crack. We've never seen grass. We've never seen the river, and it's right there, it's just across the road. And now the canteen doesn't want us. So we go. Just for a little while. A few days. We see the park, we see the river, and we come back when Mrs. Okafor has calmed down and the food's back on the shelf."

"Old Ludo said—"

"Old Ludo went out and he came back. He's fine."

"He's missing an ear."

"He's alive." She turned over to face him. In the dark her eyes were two bright beads. "Please. I don't want to go without you. But I will. I think I will. I think if I don't, I'll lie here every night for the rest of my life wondering."

Tuck thought about the fourth floor, and the winter, and a mouse who went up to see what had changed and did not come back.

Then he thought about Bramble lying on the cold concrete with her chin on the floor, breathing in the river.

"A few days," he said.

She pressed her nose to his. "A few days."

Chapter 4: Under the Roller Door

They left on Tuesday morning at a quarter to six.

Tuck had not slept. He had lain in the nest and listened to the cables and tried to remember every sound so that he could carry it with him: the hum, the tick of the hot water pipe, the faint far-off drip in the car park that came every eleven seconds. Bramble had slept like a stone and woken up singing.

They went down to the loading dock and hid behind the flattened cardboard and waited.

At a quarter to six the lorry came. They heard its engine first, a deep grumble they felt through the concrete, then the hiss of its brakes, then Kev's door slamming. Then the long hooked pole scraping at the bottom of the roller door, and the rattle, and the light.

"Morning, Kev," said Kev to himself. "Lovely morning for it, Kev."

The cold came in. Tuck's whiskers flattened against his face.

"Now," said Bramble, and she ran.

Tuck had always thought that when it came to it, his legs would refuse. They didn't. He found himself running after her across the open concrete, past the great black wheel of the lorry, past Kev's boots, his body screaming at him that he was visible, that there was nothing over his head, nothing on either side, nothing. Then the ground changed under his paws from smooth concrete to rough wet tarmac, and the smell changed from diesel and cardboard to rain and leaves and the whole wide world, and they were out.

***

They stopped under a hedge at the edge of the car park, because Bramble had stopped, and because Tuck could not have gone another step.

He had never been so cold. The air moved. That was the thing he had not understood, looking through the gap under the door: the air out here was never still. It pushed at him and pulled at his fur and found its way down to his skin. Water hung on every leaf of the hedge and fell on him in fat, freezing drops.

Bramble did not seem to feel any of it.

"Look," she said. "Tuck. Look up."

He looked up.

Above the hedge, above the car park, above the black bulk of Meridian Tower, which he saw now for the first time from the outside, and which was much taller and much plainer than he had imagined, there was the sky. It was not orange now. It was a pale, washed blue, going pink at one edge, and it went on and on, and there was nothing holding it up.

Tuck felt sick. He pressed his belly into the wet earth.

"Isn't it beautiful?" said Bramble.

"Yes," said Tuck, because she needed him to say it.

Behind them, the lorry's reversing alarm began to beep, and the great wheels began to roll back towards the hedge, and they ran again.


Chapter 5: Hafenstraße

The road was the first real test, and they nearly failed it.

Between the car park and the park lay Hafenstraße, four lanes wide, with a strip of muddy grass in the middle where a line of young trees stood in plastic sleeves. In the early morning it was not yet busy. A car would come every few seconds, then a gap, then two together, then a bus that shook the ground. Tuck and Bramble crouched at the edge of the kerb in the gutter, where the rainwater ran in a cold brown stream around their feet, and watched.

"They don't stop," said Tuck. "Old Ludo said they don't stop."

"Then we go between them."

"How do we know when?"

Bramble watched for a long time. She was counting; he could see it in the small movements of her head.

"After the bus," she said. "After the bus there's always a gap. Three times now. After the bus."

The bus came. It was enormous, a wall of red and glass and noise, and its wind knocked Tuck off his feet into the gutter water. When he scrambled up, soaked, Bramble was already on the road.

He ran after her.

The tarmac was huge. It went on and on, wet and black and stinking of oil. Somewhere to his left he heard a new engine growing louder, saw out of the corner of his eye a white light coming. He did not look. He ran. Ahead of him Bramble reached the strip of grass in the middle and disappeared into it, and he threw himself after her just as the car went past, so close that its wind rolled him over twice in the mud.

They lay in the grass at the foot of a young tree and shook.

"Two more lanes," said Bramble, when she could speak.

***

They waited for another bus. It took a long time. While they waited a pigeon landed in the grass beside them, a fat grey bird with one twisted foot, and looked at them with a red eye.

"New," said the pigeon. It was not a question.

"We're from the tower," said Bramble. "Meridian Tower. We're going to the park."

"Tower mice." The pigeon made a sound that might have been a laugh. "Fat. Soft. You won't last."

"We've lasted this far."

"You've lasted half a road," said the pigeon. "My name's Grit. I've lived on this street four years. You want advice?"

Bramble did. Tuck did not, but he listened.

"Cars, you know. Bicycles, you don't hear. Watch for bicycles. Cats, you don't hear either. The big ginger one by the bakery, behind the park, she's the worst. Duchess, the children call her. She doesn't eat what she kills. She just likes it. Stay away from the bakery." Grit cocked his head. "Bus."

The bus came. They ran, and this time the far side of the road was empty, and they made it to the kerb and up and over and into a flowerbed full of brown, dying marigolds, and beyond the flowerbed was a low iron fence, and beyond the fence was grass.

Real grass. It went on for ever.


Chapter 6: The Park

For a day, Bramble was right about everything.

The park was the most wonderful place either of them had ever seen. The grass was long and wet and full of seeds, and under the seeds there were beetles, and under the beetles there was soft dark earth that smelled like nothing in the tower. There were trees, and Bramble was right: they went up higher than anything, higher than you could see, and their leaves were turning yellow and red and falling in slow spirals that she chased like a child. There was a pond with ducks, which ignored them, and a bench where an old man ate a sandwich and dropped half of it, and a bin that smelled of crisps.

And at the far edge of the park, past the pond and down a flight of stone steps, there was the river.

They sat on the bottom step, with the brown water sliding past almost at their feet, and looked at it for a very long time. It was wider than the car park. It was wider than the road. It moved all at once, in one direction, without hurrying and without stopping, and on its far side there were more towers, a whole city of them, with the low sun shining off their glass.

"It goes all the way to the sea," said Bramble.

"How do you know?"

"I don't," she said happily. "That's the point. I don't know. I don't know anything. Isn't it wonderful?"

Tuck looked at her: wet, muddy, one ear folded back from a tumble in the flowerbed, her whiskers shining. He had never seen her so happy. He had never, he thought, seen anyone so happy.

"Yes," he said, and for the first time he meant it.

***

In the afternoon they found the cat.

Or rather, the cat found them. They were in the long grass by the fence, eating seeds, and Tuck was beginning to think that perhaps Old Ludo had exaggerated, perhaps the outside was hard but not as hard as all that, when the grass in front of him parted and there was a face.

It was the size of a whole world. It was orange and white, with eyes the colour of the light in the canteen at night, green and gold, and they were looking straight at him with an interest so complete and so calm that for a moment he could not move at all.

"Run," said Bramble, very quietly.

They ran. The cat came after them in a single soft leap that covered the ground it had taken them a breath to cross. Tuck felt the air of it on his back. Then they were under the bench, the old man's bench, in the narrow gap between the iron foot and the paving stone, pressed so hard against the metal that it hurt.

The cat sat down beside the bench. She did not seem to be in a hurry. She licked one paw, and looked at them, and licked it again.

After a long time, the old man came back to sit on the bench. The cat looked at him with disdain, stood up, and walked away across the grass with her tail high, not looking back.

"That was Duchess," whispered Bramble. "Grit said. The ginger one."

"I know who it was," said Tuck.


Chapter 7: Cold

The night was the worst thing yet.

Neither of them had known that outside, the dark came so early and so completely. In the tower, night was a matter of the lights being switched off. Here it was the whole world turning away from you. By five o'clock the sun had gone down behind the towers on the far side of the river, and the park's few lamps came on, and the warmth went out of the ground as if someone had pulled a plug.

They looked for shelter and found almost nothing. Every hole under every tree root had something in it already: a beetle, a toad, the bitter smell of a fox. In the end they crawled into a paper coffee cup that had been thrown under a hedge. It still smelled of milk and sugar, and they licked the inside of it until there was nothing left, and then they curled together at the bottom of it and waited for morning.

It did not come.

It did not come for hours. The cold came instead. It came up through the paper, and in through the opening of the cup, and it found every part of Tuck that was not pressed against Bramble and settled there. He shivered until his muscles ached and then kept shivering. He dreamed, briefly, of the duct, of the cables humming, of the hot water pipe that made your paws tingle, and woke up with his face wet.

"Tuck," said Bramble, sometime in the deepest part of the night. Her voice was very small. "Are you awake?"

"Yes."

"I'm sorry."

He didn't say anything.

"I thought it would be like the park all the time," she said. "I thought it would be like the river."

"It was, for a bit."

"Do you want to go home?"

He wanted it so much he could not speak. He wanted it the way you want breath.

"Do you?" he managed.

There was a long silence.

"Tomorrow," she said. "I want to see one more thing. Grit said there's a bakery behind the park, where they throw out the bread. I want to eat warm bread, one time, outside, in the morning. And then we go home. I promise. I'll follow you all the way back, and I won't say anything, and I won't ever ask to go anywhere again."

"Grit said to stay away from the bakery."

"Grit's a pigeon," said Bramble. "Pigeons are afraid of everything."

***

The frost came at four. Tuck felt it before he saw it: a new kind of cold, sharp and dry, that made the paper of the cup crackle. When the sky began, finally, to lighten, he pushed his nose out of the opening and saw that the whole park had turned white. Every blade of grass was furred with ice. The pond had a skin on it. Their breath smoked.

Bramble's fur was white at the tips with frost. She was shivering so hard that her teeth clicked, and when she stood up she stumbled.

"Bread," she said, through the clicking. "Warm bread. Then home."

He should have said no. He would think about that for the rest of his life: that he should have said no, and that he had not, because she had looked at him with her frost-white whiskers and her bright eyes and he had never once in their whole lives been able to say no to her.

"Then home," he said.


Chapter 8: The Bakery

The bakery was behind the park on a little side street, and they could smell it from three gardens away. It was the most beautiful smell in the world. It was better than lasagne, better than focaccia, better than the Friday Cheddar: a warm, golden, yeasty cloud that rolled out of a vent in the bakery's back wall and filled the whole frozen street.

Behind the bakery there was a yard with three big green bins, and beside the bins, on the cobbles, someone had put out a crate of yesterday's rolls. The crate had a plastic sheet over it, but the sheet had been tugged loose at one corner by the wind or by a bird, and the rolls inside were almost spilling out.

Bramble saw them and made a sound Tuck had never heard her make.

"Wait," said Tuck.

He made himself look. He looked at the top of the wall. He looked at the gap under the bins. He looked at the back door of the bakery, which stood a little open, letting out warmth and light and the sound of a radio. He looked at the shadow beside the drainpipe.

Nothing.

"Go round the edge," he said. "Like in the kitchen. Once round the edge first."

"Tuck," said Bramble, and her voice was full of laughter and frost. "I'm so cold. I'm so hungry. Please."

And she ran out across the cobbles towards the crate.

***

He would remember it, afterwards, as having been completely silent.

Grit was right: you did not hear the cat. There was no sound at all. One moment there was Bramble, small and brown and running across the frosted cobbles with her tail up, her breath smoking, towards a roll the size of her whole body; and the next moment there was a streak of orange coming down off the lid of the furthest bin, where she must have been lying flat the whole time, invisible against the green, watching.

Duchess did not chase. She did not need to. She simply arrived.

Tuck saw one paw come down. He saw Bramble roll over, and try to get up, and roll over again. He saw the cat bend her head, quite gently, almost tenderly, and pick Bramble up by the back of her neck, the way their mother had once carried them both down from the fourth floor.

Bramble made one sound.

Then the cat looked across the yard, over the mouse in her mouth, straight at Tuck.

He did not decide to run. His body decided for him. He was already moving, back along the wall, under the gate, across the garden, through the hedge, across the next garden, before any part of him that could think had caught up. Behind him there was nothing, no footsteps, no pursuit. The cat had what she wanted.

He ran until the bakery smell was gone and the frost was turning to grey slush under his feet. Then he crawled under a parked car and lay against the cold metal of its wheel, and he could not see properly, and he did not know whether that was the cold or something else.

He kept thinking: she didn't even get to taste the bread.


Chapter 9: The Way Back

He stayed under the car for most of the morning, because he did not know what else to do. People walked past on the pavement: boots, wheels, a dog on a lead that sniffed at the car and was pulled away. Nobody saw him. The world had not noticed. It went on being enormous and busy and cold, exactly as before, and Bramble was not in it.

Around noon the car's engine started above him, a terrible roar that went through his whole body, and he ran without thinking and found himself on the pavement in the open. He stood there, shaking, with the sky over his head and nothing around him, and for the first time since the bakery a single thought came clearly.

Home.

He knew the way. He had not known he knew it, but he did. He had watched every step on the way out, the way he watched everything: the flowerbed with the brown marigolds, the iron fence, the park, Hafenstraße, the strip of grass with the young trees, the hedge, the car park, the roller door. He had been so busy being afraid that he had memorised the whole of the world.

***

He crossed the park along its edge, in the long grass by the fence, never in the open. He did not go near the bench. He did not look at the river.

At Hafenstraße he waited for the bus. It took a very long time, and while he waited it began to rain, a thin, icy rain that soaked through his fur in minutes. When the bus came he ran. Halfway across the first two lanes something went past him with a hiss and a rattle and a voice shouting, and a wheel as thin as a knife went over the tip of his tail. A bicycle. You don't hear bicycles. He reached the middle strip and lay in the mud, panting, his tail throbbing.

Grit was there, under the same young tree.

"One," said the pigeon, looking at him. Then, after a moment, more quietly: "The bakery?"

Tuck didn't answer. He didn't need to.

"I told her," said Grit. He shook out his feathers against the rain. "I always tell them. They never listen. The ones that listen don't come out in the first place." He tilted his head at the road. "Bus."

Tuck ran.

***

He reached the hedge at the edge of the car park as the light was going. The roller door was shut. Of course it was shut. It only opened at a quarter to six in the morning, when Kev came. Tuck had known that. He had simply not thought about it, the way Bramble never thought about the gap behind the boiler.

He crept down the ramp to the door and pressed his face to the gap underneath. It was there, the gap, the same as it had always been. But the gap had only ever been for looking. A mouse on his own, with no one to stand on, could not reach the underside of the door to squeeze through.

He tried anyway. He tried for an hour. He tried until his claws were bleeding and his nose was raw from the concrete, and then he lay down in the corner of the ramp, against the wall, out of the worst of the rain, and waited for a quarter to six.

It was the longest night of his life. Longer than the night in the coffee cup. He was alone now, and nobody pressed against his side, and there was no Bramble to ask him if he was awake.

At a quarter to six, the lorry came.

He heard the engine first, the deep familiar grumble in the concrete. Then the brakes, the door, the long hooked pole scraping at the bottom of the roller door, the rattle, and the light. Warm air came pouring out over him, smelling of diesel and cardboard and, very faintly, from far up inside the building, of Mrs. Okafor's onions.

"Morning, Kev," said Kev. "Freezing, Kev. Absolutely freezing."

Tuck ran in between his boots. Kev looked down, and Tuck saw him see.

"Oh," said Kev. "Hello, mate. You look like I feel."

He did not stamp. He did not shout. He stepped carefully to one side, and Tuck ran past the lorry's great black wheel and across the smooth, dry, beautiful concrete, past the flattened cardboard, into the dark at the back of the loading dock, and through the crack in the wall.

Behind him he heard Kev say, to nobody, "Better not tell Brandt about you."


Epilogue

The duct was warm.

That was the first thing. He had been afraid, on the long cold way back, that it would not be, that somehow the warmth had been something Bramble had carried with her. But the cables still hummed, the hot water pipe still made his paws tingle, and when he crawled through the gap behind the loose panel the nest was exactly as they had left it: the shredded payslips, the blue lanyard, the stuffing from the chair. Her smell was still in it.

He lay down in the hollow where she always slept and stayed there for a long time.

***

It was hunger that got him up, in the end. Two days in the park, one night in a coffee cup, one night on the ramp. He had eaten a few seeds and the inside of a paper cup. His belly hurt.

He did not have the strength to climb the wall to the kitchen. But he did not need to.

Halfway along the service corridor, where the pipe came down from the ceiling, there was something new. It was a little black box, square, with a lid, with a label on it in letters he could not read, and two round doorways at either end, exactly the size of a mouse. It had not been there when they left. It smelled of plastic, and of a human he did not know, and very strongly, very sweetly, of something else.

Tuck hesitated at the doorway. He went round the box once, the way he always did. He checked the dark beneath the pipe and the corners of the corridor. Nothing. No cat. No Duster. Only the hum of the cables, the drip in the car park every eleven seconds, the deep and breathing quiet of the tower at night.

He went in.

Inside the box, on a little spike, were small blue blocks. He nibbled the corner of one. It tasted like cereal, and honey, and something faintly bitter underneath that he did not mind at all. It was the best thing he had eaten since the lasagne. It was better than the lasagne.

There was another box further along the corridor. And one by the boiler room. And one, he found later, in the loading dock, right beside the flattened cardboard where he and Bramble used to hide. There were blue blocks in all of them. There was more food than he had ever seen in one place, more than the conference biscuits, more than he could eat in a month.

Mrs. Okafor, he thought. She had calmed down. She had forgiven them. She had cooked too much again, the way she always did, and this time she had brought it down to them herself.

He ate until his belly was round and tight. Then he carried one more blue block back along the corridor, through the crack, along the warm pipe, into the duct, and laid it in the hollow where Bramble used to sleep, because she would have loved it, and it seemed wrong to eat all of it without her.

Then he curled up beside it in the warm dark, with the cables humming their ordinary evening hum, and closed his eyes.

He was home. He had been out into the world, and he had seen the sky and the grass and the river that went all the way to the sea, and he had come back, and nothing out there could reach him now. Tomorrow there would be more blue blocks. The day after, more again.

For the first time since the bakery, Tuck slept deeply, and he was not afraid.
